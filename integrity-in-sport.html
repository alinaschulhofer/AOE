Integrity in Sport: The Psychology of Good Governance

How Interpersonal Dynamics Shape Culture, Leadership, and Institutional Change

Dr. Alina Schulhofer

Across international sport, issues of integrity, fairness, and equality have become more than abstract aspirations. Transparency, accountability, athlete representation, and ethical decision-making are increasingly formalized through governance frameworks designed to strengthen trust and integrity across the ecosystem.

These efforts represent tremendous progress. Teams and organizations on the international stage promote initiatives that reflect a growing understanding that good governance cannot simply be advertised as an institutional value, but that these ideas must be translated into concrete structures, standards, and measurable practices. While this is an important evolution, it also raises another question: What does it actually take for a team or organization to change?

Certainly, policies can be amended. Independent oversight can be introduced, and new mechanisms for transparency and accountability can be established on paper. Yet teams and organizations are not simply composed of policies and procedures: they are also human systems, shaped by the individuals within them, the relationships between them, and the historical patterns of behavior that have survived, often for decades. Tradition tends to prevail, even when it's dysfunctional.

Change is difficult: individually, and especially collectively and culturally. From a psychological perspective, resistance to change is often rooted in anxiety and fears of loss, even when the proposed changes are well-intentioned. Change means uncertainty and can trigger deep fears in individuals and groups alike, not infrequently related to identity, status, competence, belonging, or control.

We often resort to familiar patterns because we prefer the status quo and the equilibrium it offers. Meaningful organizational shifts can challenge established roles, redistribute power, disrupt familiar ways of behaving, and call into question long-held assumptions and traditions. These factors are not merely structural or procedural, but psychological, emotional, and relational in nature.

It follows, then, that if the ultimate goal of governance reform is long-lasting, meaningful cultural change, structural efforts are only part of the equation. Culture can never change without the people in it changing, and people do not always respond to change rationally or predictably. This is true even when they support, in principle, the ideas and goals behind it.

Understanding integrity in sport therefore requires us to consider not only the structures that make good governance possible, but also the psychology of the people and organizations being asked to enact it.

An organization can introduce new mechanisms intended to strengthen athlete representation. On paper, athletes may now have a seat at the table and a formal opportunity to contribute to decisions. But the existence of that structure doesn't necessarily mean that an athlete feels psychologically safe enough to disagree with a leader who may also hold considerable influence over their career and future opportunities. Nor does it mean that those in positions of authority will necessarily be able to receive disagreement without becoming dismissive or defensive.

The same tensions exist within many of the principles associated with institutional reform. An organization can create mechanisms for honest feedback, but its leaders must still be able to tolerate perspectives that may challenge their own. Leaders can commit themselves to transparency, but this may also require them to tolerate scrutiny. Accountability may require someone to acknowledge that a decision was wrong, or that a long-standing way of operating is no longer effective. Greater equality and inclusivity may require those accustomed to holding authority to share some degree of power or control.

Creating institutional mechanisms for integrity is different from creating the psychological conditions in which those mechanisms can actually function. Sporting systems also tend to be hierarchical, with individuals sometimes remaining within the same ecosystem for decades and moving between roles as athletes, coaches, officials, administrators, or organizational leaders. Established ways of operating can easily become intertwined with relationships or status. Meaningful reform may therefore require people not only to adopt new practices, but to reconsider something they themselves helped to build.

These factors are particularly important for those in positions of leadership. Organizations are not only structural systems, but emotional and relational ones, and leaders are not neutral observers standing outside of them. Their personalities, relationships, fears, and blind spots inevitably influence how they exercise authority, respond to criticism, interpret disagreement, and make decisions. Over time, the psychological patterns of those with the greatest influence become embedded within the culture of the organizations they lead.

A leader who experiences disagreement as threatening, for instance, may consciously value open communication while inadvertently creating an environment in which people learn not to challenge them. A coach who struggles to acknowledge mistakes may endorse accountability while creating a culture in which responsibility is habitually deflected elsewhere. Someone whose sense of competence depends upon maintaining control may genuinely support greater participation yet struggle when meaningful participation requires relinquishing some power.

These contradictions don't necessarily reflect deliberate hypocrisy. Rather, they illustrate a fundamental difficulty inherent in integrity itself: we can consciously believe in a value while simultaneously behaving in ways that undermine it, particularly when acting in accordance with that value requires us to confront something uncomfortable within ourselves.

Self-awareness, therefore, is not merely a personal-development exercise for leaders. In positions of significant authority, it becomes an organizational responsibility: the ways leaders respond to difference, challenge, criticism, and power inevitably shape the cultures around them. Organizational culture is revealed not only by the values an institution professes, but by what happens when those values become psychologically difficult to uphold.

Transparency is relatively easy when the information being shared is favorable. Accountability is easier when responsibility belongs to someone else. Athlete representation is uncomplicated when athletes agree with those already in power, and openness to feedback is comfortable when the feedback confirms what we already believe.

The real test of integrity lies in the following: do we possess the psychological, emotional, and relational skills to act in accordance with our principles, even when they require us to tolerate discomfort? Can we maintain our professional and institutional values when transparency exposes something we would rather not see, when accountability requires acknowledging our own mistakes, or when feedback challenges our understanding of ourselves?

If organizations are serious about creating lasting cultures of integrity, the work can't end with establishing better policies or governance frameworks. Institutional development must also involve human development: leaders need the capacity to reflect on their own behavior and recognize how their psychological patterns shape the systems around them, while organizations must create cultures in which people can question established practices, acknowledge mistakes, and engage honestly with uncomfortable realities.

New frameworks can provide the structural architecture for greater integrity, transparency, and equality. But policies alone don't create culture, and institutions can't evolve independently of the people within them. If integrity is to become something more than a principle written into governance frameworks, the psychological and relational dimensions of organizational change must become part of the conversation. Ultimately, changing the structures of an ecosystem also requires a willingness to examine, and change, ourselves.

Creating institutional mechanisms for integrity is different from creating the psychological conditions in which those mechanisms can actually function.
