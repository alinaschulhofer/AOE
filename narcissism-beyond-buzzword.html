Narcissism: Beyond the Buzzword

Rather than being just a "character flaw," narcissism is a spectrum of traits that can be adaptive when healthy, and destructive when pathological.

Dr. Alina Schulhofer

"Narcissist" has become one of the most frequently used — and frequently misused — words in contemporary psychological discourse. On social media, in popular self-help content, and even in casual conversation, it is deployed as a label for anyone who seems self-centered, difficult, or unwilling to take responsibility. This flattening of the concept has consequences: it obscures the genuine complexity of narcissism as a psychological phenomenon, and it can prevent people from engaging with it in ways that are actually useful.

As a psychologist, I want to offer something more nuanced — a framework that takes narcissism seriously as a clinical concept while also recognizing that it exists on a continuum, and that some degree of what we might call narcissistic functioning is not only normal but necessary.

Narcissism as a Foundation for Self-Esteem

In psychoanalytic theory, narcissism refers not to vanity or selfishness, but to the investment of psychic energy in the self — the degree to which we value, protect, and attend to our own experience. In this sense, healthy narcissism is foundational to psychological wellbeing. It is what allows us to maintain a stable sense of self-worth, to set appropriate limits, to pursue our desires and ambitions, and to feel entitled to take up space in the world.

Otto Kernberg, one of the foremost theorists of narcissism, distinguishes between normal narcissism — which involves a stable, realistically positive sense of self — and pathological narcissism, which involves a grandiose self-structure built on the denial of dependency, the devaluation of others, and a fragile internal world defended by elaborate psychological mechanisms (Kernberg, 1975). Nancy McWilliams similarly emphasizes that narcissistic pathology is characterized not by excess self-love, but by a deep underlying shame and a compensatory need for external validation (McWilliams, 2011).

Healthy vs. Pathological Narcissism

The distinction between healthy and pathological narcissism is clinically important — and practically relevant, because the two can look superficially similar from the outside while being fundamentally different in their internal structure and relational impact.

A healthy narcissistic individual can take genuine pride in their achievements without requiring constant admiration. They can tolerate criticism without being devastated by it. They can feel competitive without needing to destroy the competition. They can be moved by others' experiences — genuinely empathic — while also maintaining a clear sense of their own needs and boundaries. Their self-esteem, while robust, is not brittle.

Pathological narcissism, by contrast, is characterized by a fragility that is often invisible from the outside. Beneath the grandiosity and the confident exterior lies a deep vulnerability — a terror of being ordinary, of being exposed as inadequate, of needing others in ways that feel shameful. The defensive structures erected to manage this vulnerability — idealization and devaluation, entitlement, exploitation, rage when slighted — are not expressions of strength. They are expressions of profound inner precariousness.

Narcissism as a Continuum

Rather than a binary category — narcissist or not — narcissism is best understood as a continuum. Most people have some narcissistic traits; the question is whether those traits are flexible and adaptive, or rigid and destructive. Context matters enormously: traits that are mildly narcissistic in one setting may become significantly problematic in another, particularly in positions of power where the usual social constraints on behavior are weakened and the individual's impact on others is magnified.

High-performance environments are particularly relevant here. The same traits that can fuel extraordinary achievement — confidence, ambition, a belief in one's own exceptional ability — can, in their more pathological forms, create significant harm: to organizations, to teams, to intimate partners, and ultimately to the individual themselves. Understanding where a person sits on this continuum, and what drives their narcissistic functioning, is essential to any meaningful work with them.

Why This Matters: Social Media and Oversimplification

The social media discourse around narcissism is problematic not because it raises the topic — awareness of narcissistic harm is genuinely important — but because it tends to treat narcissism as a fixed, static category rather than a dynamic psychological pattern. When we label someone a "narcissist" and consider the conversation closed, we foreclose the possibility of understanding the pain and developmental history that underlies their behavior, the degree to which change might be possible, and our own contribution to the dynamic.

This is not to minimize genuine harm. Relationships with pathologically narcissistic individuals can be genuinely damaging, and naming that damage is important. But the binary label often serves to organize our own emotional experience rather than to understand the other person with any depth.

Clinical Work With Narcissism

Working clinically with narcissistic presentations requires considerable skill, patience, and a genuine capacity to hold the complexity of the person's inner world. Pathologically narcissistic individuals rarely present for therapy in a state of genuine self-reflection — they are more likely to arrive in crisis, or under external pressure, with their defenses intact and their motivation for change ambivalent at best.

The work, when it is possible, is slow and relational. It involves creating a therapeutic relationship that is stable enough to hold the inevitable storms — the devaluation, the testing, the withdrawal — and yet warm enough to offer a genuine experience of being seen and valued without being exploited. Over time, with a sufficiently skilled and patient clinician, some individuals are able to access the underlying vulnerability that drives their narcissistic functioning, and to begin building a more authentic and less defended relationship with themselves and others. This work is among the most challenging in clinical psychology — and among the most meaningful.
