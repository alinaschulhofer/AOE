Looking Beyond Success and Failure

Neither triumph nor disaster need define you — unless you allow them to.

Dr. Alina Schulhofer
"If you can meet with Triumph and Disaster, and treat those two impostors just the same…" — Rudyard Kipling, If

Kipling's famous lines have endured for over a century because they capture something psychologically profound: both success and failure, in their extreme forms, can become emotional storms — capable of destabilizing us if we don't have the inner resources to meet them with steadiness. The real question is not whether you will experience triumph or disaster — you will experience both. The real question is: who do you become in response?

We tend to think of resilience as the ability to recover from failure. But there is an equally important and often overlooked capacity: the ability to handle success with grace. Because success, too, can knock us off our center — flooding us with pressure, expectation, and the fear that we may not be able to sustain what we've achieved.

How We Handle Failure

Failure carries an enormous psychological charge. For high achievers in particular, failure is not merely an outcome — it can feel like a verdict on their worth. When something goes wrong, the inner critic often moves in quickly, broadcasting narratives of inadequacy, shame, or doom. The danger is not the failure itself, but the story we tell about it.

When failure is internalized as identity — "I am a failure" rather than "I failed at this" — it erodes the foundation upon which healthy striving is built. Chronic self-criticism, avoidance, and withdrawal are common responses that feel protective but ultimately deepen the wound. The antidote is not toxic positivity or forced reframing. It is something more honest: the ability to sit with what happened, to learn what can be learned, and to let the rest go.

How We Handle Success

Success presents its own psychological challenges — ones that are far less discussed. After a significant achievement, many high performers describe a surprising emptiness, a sense of anticlimax, or an anxiety about maintaining what they have built. The goalpost moves. The pressure intensifies. The fear of being exposed as unworthy of one's success — what psychologists call impostor phenomenon — can intensify precisely when external indicators suggest things are going well.

Others respond to success with a kind of inflation: an overidentification with the win that sets them up for a harder fall when the next setback comes. Success can also create relational stress — envy from peers, elevated expectations from those around us, and a quiet loneliness that can accompany being at the top.

True Resilience

True resilience is not about bouncing back quickly — it is about maintaining a stable sense of self across both peaks and valleys. It requires what psychologists call ego strength: the capacity to witness your own experience without being entirely swept away by it. This is not detachment. It is a kind of grounded presence that can hold both the joy of triumph and the pain of defeat without losing the thread of who you are.

Athletes, executives, and artists who sustain excellence over time tend to share one quality above others: they do not allow their identity to collapse into their results. They care deeply about what they do — and they have cultivated an internal home that is not entirely dependent on external validation.

The work of deepening your relationship to success and failure is ultimately the work of deepening your relationship with yourself.

The Inner Work

Learning to meet triumph and disaster more evenly is not a passive process. It requires deliberate inner work: examining the beliefs we hold about what success and failure mean, understanding the emotional patterns that get activated by each, and building a relationship with ourselves that is not entirely contingent on outcome.

This might look like developing a regular reflective practice — journaling, meditation, or honest conversations with a therapist or coach. It might mean building stronger roots in values and relationships that exist outside of achievement. It often involves grief work: mourning the losses that failure brings, and also mourning the weight that excessive attachment to success creates.

In my work with high performers, this is some of the most meaningful territory we enter together. Because when people begin to uncouple their identity from their results, something shifts — not just emotionally, but in the quality and consistency of their performance itself.

Whether through therapy, coaching, or intentional self-reflection, the work of deepening your relationship to success and failure is ultimately the work of deepening your relationship with yourself.
