The Power of Meaning in High-Performance Systems

High achievement does not guarantee inner purpose, happiness, or meaning.

Dr. Alina Schulhofer

In May 2024, Scottie Scheffler — ranked No. 1 in the world in professional golf — stood at a press conference following yet another tournament win and asked, quietly and with evident sincerity: "What's the point of it all?" It was a moment that caught many observers off guard. Here was the best golfer on the planet, accumulating victories at a historic pace, appearing to grapple publicly with a question that performance culture rarely makes room for: whether all of this striving and succeeding actually means anything.

Scheffler later elaborated, saying that winning felt "awesome" — but only for about two minutes. Then the feeling passed, and he was left with himself again. It is a confession that many high achievers recognize privately but rarely voice aloud, because the culture of high performance is not well designed to hold it.

Meaning as an Inner System

What Scheffler was articulating — perhaps without using this language — is the difference between achievement and meaning. Achievement is external: a result, a ranking, a record. Meaning is internal: the sense that what one is doing matters, connects to something larger, and aligns with one's deepest values and sense of self.

Existential psychologists have long recognized meaning as a fundamental human need — not a luxury or a philosophical abstraction, but a psychological necessity. Viktor Frankl, writing from his experience in Nazi concentration camps, argued that human beings can endure almost anything if they have a strong enough sense of why (Frankl, 1963). Irvin Yalom, building on this tradition, described meaninglessness as one of the core existential anxieties — a source of profound inner suffering that no amount of external accomplishment can resolve (Yalom, 1980).

What this means for high performers is significant: the systems that sustain excellence cannot be built from performance metrics and winning alone. They require a deeper architecture — one in which the individual knows not only what they are pursuing, but why it matters to them at a level that goes beyond accolades and external recognition.

The most durable performers are not those who want it most — they are those who know why they want it, and who have built a life that reflects that understanding.

When the System Runs on Empty

Scheffler's two-minute window is not unusual. Research on hedonic adaptation consistently shows that the emotional impact of positive events fades far more quickly than we anticipate. We return, again and again, to a psychological baseline — and if that baseline is characterized by a sense of emptiness or purposelessness, no amount of winning will fill it.

This is not a performance problem in the conventional sense. It is a meaning problem. And it is one that coaches, trainers, and performance consultants who focus exclusively on technical or tactical development are not equipped to address. The individual may have every external advantage — elite coaching, optimal conditioning, sophisticated analytics — and still feel, at the end of the day, that something essential is missing.

Pratt and Ashforth (2003) have written about the distinction between "meaningfulness of work" — whether what one does feels significant — and "meaningfulness at work" — whether one feels a sense of belonging and mattering within a broader context. Both are necessary, and both can be absent even in environments of extraordinary achievement.

Bringing Purpose Back Into Performance

The work of reconnecting performance to meaning is not simple, and it is not a one-time intervention. It requires ongoing reflection, often supported by a therapist or coach who can hold the space for the kinds of questions that high-performance culture tends to suppress. Questions like: What am I actually doing this for? Who am I when I am not competing or producing? What would I want my work to have meant, if I looked back on it at the end of my life?

Research on organizational behavior suggests that leaders who communicate a compelling sense of purpose — who connect the work of their team to outcomes that genuinely matter — unlock higher levels of discretionary effort, creativity, and resilience (Carton, 2018). This is not motivational rhetoric. It is a recognition that human beings perform at their best when they understand and believe in what they are doing.

For individual athletes and executives, this means doing the deeper work of clarifying and cultivating a personal sense of purpose — not as an add-on to the performance program, but as a foundational element of it. The most durable performers are not those who want it most — they are those who know why they want it, and who have built a life that reflects that understanding.

When Individual Systems Shape Collective Ones

There is also an organizational dimension to this conversation. When leaders lack a genuine sense of meaning in their work, that absence propagates outward. Teams pick up on the disengagement of those above them, often before it is consciously visible. An executive who is going through the motions — achieving targets without being moved by the work — creates a subtle but pervasive cultural flatness that no engagement survey fully captures.

Conversely, leaders who are genuinely animated by purpose — who can articulate with conviction why what they are doing matters — create environments that are qualitatively different. People want to work for them, not merely because of the compensation or the prestige, but because the work feels like it means something. Purpose at the individual level becomes culture at the collective level.

This is one of the reasons why leadership development programs that focus exclusively on skills and behaviors miss something important. Skill without meaning is competence without direction. The leaders who truly transform their organizations are those who have done the inner work of understanding what they are here to do — and who bring that clarity, consistently and with integrity, to everything they build.

If you are producing at the highest level but the results feel empty or fleeting, the solution may be to build, or rebuild, a system of values and meaning that connects what you do to why it matters.
