Understanding the Gifts (and Challenges) of Neurodivergence in High Performance

The very qualities that drive achievement may also increase the risk of psychological strain — without recognition and support.

Dr. Alina Schulhofer

A growing number of adults are receiving diagnoses — or seeking evaluation — for conditions such as ADHD, autism spectrum disorder, dyslexia, and other forms of neurodivergence, often for the first time in midlife. Many of them are high achievers: executives, entrepreneurs, athletes, artists, and professionals who have spent decades developing elaborate compensatory strategies to manage cognitive and sensory differences they may not have had language for until now.

This late-diagnosis phenomenon is not coincidental. Many neurodivergent individuals are exceptionally intelligent, motivated, and capable of extraordinary performance in domains that align with their particular cognitive strengths. The same qualities that drive their achievement — an intense capacity for focus, pattern recognition, creative thinking, and hyperfixation on areas of deep interest — can mask the very real difficulties they experience in other areas. Until, often, the mask begins to slip.

The Importance of Accurate Professional Diagnosis

Before anything else, it bears emphasizing that neurodivergence is not a self-diagnosis. The proliferation of social media content about ADHD, autism, and related conditions has increased public awareness — which is, on balance, a positive development. But awareness and diagnosis are not the same thing, and conflating them can lead to both over-identification and missed cases of genuine complexity.

A thorough neuropsychological evaluation, conducted by a qualified clinician, is the only reliable path to an accurate picture of an individual's cognitive profile. This matters not only for access to appropriate support and accommodations, but because the conditions themselves — and their presentations — vary enormously. ADHD in a high-IQ adult looks different than ADHD in a child with average intelligence. Autism in a woman who has spent decades masking may look entirely different from the classic presentations described in older diagnostic literature. Without professional evaluation, these nuances are easily missed.

Common Neurodivergent Traits in High-Achieving Contexts

While each individual's profile is unique, certain traits associated with neurodivergence appear with notable frequency in high-performance populations. These include:

  • Hyperfocus. The capacity to concentrate intensely on a domain of passionate interest for extended periods — a significant asset in fields that reward deep expertise and sustained effort.
  • Pattern recognition. An unusual ability to detect structure, connection, and possibility in complex systems — valuable in fields such as finance, science, technology, and strategic leadership.
  • Creative and divergent thinking. A tendency to approach problems from unexpected angles, generating novel solutions that more conventionally wired thinkers may not consider.
  • High sensitivity. Heightened responsiveness to sensory, emotional, and interpersonal information — which can support empathy, attunement, and aesthetic sensitivity, but also increase vulnerability to overwhelm.
  • Intense drive and perfectionism. A powerful inner compulsion toward excellence that can fuel extraordinary output — and that, without appropriate support, can also fuel anxiety, burnout, and chronic self-criticism.
  • Unconventional working styles. Difficulty with rigid structures, routines, and conventional workflows — which may lead to underperformance in environments that prize compliance over output, even when the individual's actual capabilities are exceptional.
  • Executive function variability. Inconsistency in planning, organization, time management, and task initiation — which can create significant professional and personal challenges that bear no relationship to the individual's intelligence or dedication.
Cognitive diversity is not merely a matter of inclusion — it is a driver of innovation, resilience, and organizational excellence.

The Risk of Social Media Oversimplification

The current cultural moment around neurodivergence is in many respects liberating — offering language, community, and validation to people who have spent years feeling different without knowing why. But it also carries risks. When neurodivergence becomes an identity category defined primarily by relatable content and shared experience rather than clinical understanding, it can obscure the very real heterogeneity within these populations, and reduce complex neurological profiles to a set of charming quirks.

For high performers in particular, this oversimplification can be costly. The challenges associated with neurodivergence — executive dysfunction, emotional dysregulation, sensory overwhelm, difficulties with sustained attention in non-preferred tasks — are real and deserve serious support, not just social recognition. Understanding one's profile at a clinical level allows for targeted, evidence-based strategies that social media content, however well-intentioned, cannot provide (Nerenberg, 2020).

Organizational Implications: Toward Cognitive Diversity

At the organizational level, there is growing recognition that neurodivergent individuals represent an underutilized talent pool — and that environments designed exclusively for neurotypical ways of working systematically exclude people whose contributions could be transformative (Armstrong, 2017). Companies that have intentionally sought neurodivergent talent — particularly in technical, creative, and analytical roles — report not only improved performance in those domains, but broader cultural benefits from increased cognitive diversity.

This does not mean lowering standards or creating two-tiered systems of expectation. It means examining which structural features of the work environment are truly necessary for performance, and which are simply artifacts of how workplaces have historically been organized. Flexibility in working hours, remote work options, written rather than verbal communication of key information, quiet spaces for focused work, and clear and explicit expectations are examples of accommodations that benefit neurodivergent employees while imposing minimal cost on the organization — and that often improve the experience of neurotypical employees as well (Doyle, 2020).

The organizations that will lead in the coming years are those that understand cognitive diversity not merely as a matter of inclusion, but as a driver of innovation, resilience, and organizational excellence.
