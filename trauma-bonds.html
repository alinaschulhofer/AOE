Why We Can't Let Go: The Psychology of Trauma Bonds

The pull we feel toward those who wound us is not destiny — it is a pattern we can come to understand.

Dr. Alina Schulhofer

You know this person is not good for you. Your friends have told you. Your body has told you. Some part of you has known it for a long time. And yet you can't let go. You return — to the conversation, to the relationship, to the hope that this time it will be different. You feel the pull even when you are fully aware of the pain it causes. Why?

This is not a weakness of character. It is not naivety, or poor judgment, or a simple failure to "value yourself." It is the result of a set of powerful psychological and neurobiological processes that, once understood, can begin to be worked with. The language many people reach for is "trauma bonding" — a term that has entered popular culture with considerable force. But as with many psychological concepts that migrate from clinical settings into public discourse, its meaning has been stretched and simplified in ways that sometimes obscure more than they reveal.

Trauma Bonding vs. Its Pop-Culture Meaning

Clinically, trauma bonding refers to a specific pattern that emerges in the context of ongoing abuse or coercive control — a powerful emotional attachment that forms between a person and their abuser, often characterized by cycles of harm and repair that create an intense, confused bond. It was originally observed in hostage situations, where captives could develop strong emotional attachments to their captors — a phenomenon sometimes called Stockholm syndrome.

In popular usage, "trauma bond" has come to describe almost any intense, painful, or difficult-to-end relationship. This broadening has its uses — it gives language to experiences that many people recognize — but it also carries the risk of pathologizing normal human complexity and obscuring the specific mechanisms that make certain attachments so hard to break. Understanding those mechanisms is what makes healing possible.

Repetition Compulsion

One of the most important of these mechanisms is what Freud called the repetition compulsion: the unconscious drive to recreate familiar relational patterns, even when those patterns are painful. This is not masochism. It is the psyche's attempt to master old wounds by replaying them — to finally get it right, to receive the love or repair that was not available the first time, to resolve what remains unresolved.

The partner who reminds us, at some level we may not consciously register, of a critical or unavailable parent — the dynamic that replicates the emotional texture of our earliest attachments — can exert an almost magnetic pull. Not because we are drawn to pain, but because the familiar, however painful, is what the psyche recognizes as home. We return not in spite of the suffering, but because of the deep familiarity of the pattern it enacts.

When we understand that the pull we feel is not destiny, we can reclaim our power to choose differently.

Childhood Attachment and Nervous System Confusion

Attachment theory offers another lens. When children are raised by caregivers who are also sources of fear, pain, or unpredictability — what researchers call "frightening and frightened" caregiving — they develop what is known as disorganized attachment. They are caught in an impossible bind: the person they need for safety is also the person who frightens them. The result is a nervous system that has learned to associate love with danger, closeness with pain, and attachment with ambivalence.

Carried into adult relationships, this pattern can manifest as an intense attraction to people who replicate the push-pull dynamics of early caregiving — people who are compelling and unavailable, loving and hurtful, present and withdrawn. The nervous system recognizes the pattern not as harmful, but as familiar. And familiarity, even of the most painful kind, can feel like safety.

Intermittent Reinforcement and Oxytocin

There is also a neurobiological dimension. Relationships characterized by alternating warmth and withdrawal, closeness and rejection, create what behavioral psychologists call intermittent reinforcement — a pattern of unpredictable reward that, research consistently shows, produces stronger and more persistent behavioral responses than consistent reward. The moments of connection and warmth become intensely potent, partly because they are unpredictable and partly because they offer relief from the anxiety of the intervals between them.

Oxytocin — often called the bonding hormone — is released not only during moments of positive connection, but also in the aftermath of stress and conflict. This means that the cycle of rupture and repair that characterizes many difficult relationships may, at a neurochemical level, actually strengthen the bond rather than weakening it. The body interprets the reunion, the making-up, the return of warmth, as deeply meaningful — regardless of what preceded it.

Breaking the Cycle: Beginning With Awareness

Understanding these mechanisms does not immediately dissolve their power. But it does change the quality of the struggle. When you can see the pattern clearly — when you understand that the pull you feel has roots that predate this relationship, that your nervous system is responding to familiarity rather than to genuine safety — you gain a foothold for choice that was not previously available.

Some questions worth sitting with:

  • What does this relationship remind me of? What earlier dynamic does it replicate?
  • Am I drawn to this person because they are genuinely good for me, or because they feel familiar in ways that have more to do with my history than with the present?
  • What would it mean about me — about my worth, my lovability, my adequacy — if I were to truly let this go?

That last question often reveals the deeper adhesive. We stay not only because we love the other person, but because leaving would mean confronting something about ourselves that we are not yet ready to face.

Self-Regulation and the Path Forward

Healing from a trauma bond involves building the capacity for self-regulation — the ability to tolerate the anxiety and grief of separation without being flooded by them. This is not primarily a cognitive task. It is a somatic and relational one. It requires learning to recognize and respond to the nervous system's distress signals, to develop the internal resources to self-soothe rather than automatically seeking regulation through the familiar (and familiar-feeling) other.

This is work that benefits enormously from professional support. Therapy — particularly modalities that attend to both relational patterns and somatic experience — offers not only a space for insight, but a new relational experience: one in which it is safe to be vulnerable, in which repair after rupture is genuinely possible, and in which the therapist's consistent presence creates a different template for what relationship can be. Through the transference — the way the therapist inevitably comes to embody elements of earlier significant relationships — old patterns can be seen, felt, and gradually reworked in real time.

When we understand that the pull we feel is not destiny, we can reclaim our power to choose differently. When we stop endlessly chasing connection and repair in the same places that hurt us, healing can begin.
